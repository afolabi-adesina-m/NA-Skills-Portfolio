1. Supply Chain Fundamentals

SCF

Foundations of how goods, information, and cash move: roles (supplier → plant → DC → customer), inventory drivers (cycle stock, safety stock, pipeline), replenishment math (EOQ, reorder point, CSL), ABC prioritization, and turns / days-of-supply. This vocabulary underpins design, dynamics, analytics — and the working-capital story hiring managers care about.

Skills demonstrated

  • Size EOQ, reorder point, and safety stock from demand, cost, and service targets
  • Classify SKUs (ABC) and set turns / days-of-supply targets by class
  • Connect inventory policy to stockout cost vs carrying cost tradeoffs

Sample inventory problems

Problem A — EOQ + orders per year (Mississauga CPG / retail SKU)

Retail · CPG · DC replenishment Mississauga DC

Statement: Annual demand 24,000 cases. Order / setup cost $180. Unit cost $28; holding rate 16%/yr → H = $4.48/case/yr (≈ $4.50). What EOQ minimizes order + holding cost, and how many orders per year?

Approach: EOQ = √(2DS/H). Orders/year = D/Q. Avg cycle stock = Q/2. Same pattern for manufacturing lot sizes and 3PL inbound waves.

Problem B — Reorder point & cycle service level (Oakville → Toronto)

Logistics · Retail · Manufacturing Oakville DC · 95% CSL

Statement: Daily demand μ = 60, σ = 18. Lead time 5 days (GTA carrier). Target cycle service level 95% (z ≈ 1.65). EOQ from Problem A ≈ 1,386 cases. What safety stock and reorder point?

Approach: σL = σ√L; SS = z·σL; ROP = μL + SS. CSL is the probability of no stockout in a replenishment cycle — used in retail, MRO, and plant-to-DC policies.

Problem C — ABC classification (Mississauga DC assortment)

Retail · CPG · Industrial Pareto / inventory focus

Statement: Eight SKUs at Mississauga DC with annual $ usage below. Classify A / B / C so planners put tight control on the vital few and lighter review on the trivial many.

Approach: Rank by annual usage $; cumulative % of value → A (~top 70–80%), B (next ~15–20%), C (remainder). Policy intensity follows class.

Problem D — Inventory turns, days of supply & stockout tradeoff

Working capital · Service Oakville finished goods

Statement: Oakville FG average inventory $1.85M; annual COGS $11.2M. Cutting average inventory 12% (leaner turns) raises expected stockout / expedite cost from $95k to $180k/yr while saving carrying cost at 18% of inventory. Is the cut worth it?

Approach: Turns = COGS / Avg inventory; DOS = 365 / Turns. Net = carrying savings − incremental service cost. Same tradeoff for retail backroom and spare-parts cribs.

2. Supply Chain Design

SCD

Structural choices: where to put plants and DCs, how to allocate capacity, which contracts and sourcing portfolios to use, and how to set medium-term production rates (aggregate planning). Design decisions lock in cost-to-serve for years — critical for Canada–US Great Lakes networks where border friction and corridor density matter.

Skills demonstrated

  • Compare level, chase, and hybrid aggregate plans (workforce vs inventory tradeoffs)
  • Reason about multi-echelon network / facility roles (plant, DC, cross-dock)
  • Connect design choices to service radius and landed cost

Sample problems

Problem A — Aggregate plan for an Oakville plant (level vs chase vs hybrid)

Manufacturing · CPG · Industrial Seasonal NA demand

Statement: A GTA plant faces seasonal demand over 6 months (units below). Regular capacity is flexible via overtime / temps (chase) or inventory build (level). Compare level (smooth production), chase (match demand), and hybrid (limited overtime + inventory). Which minimizes total cost of production, inventory, and workforce change?

Approach: Build monthly balance sheets: production, ending inventory, hires/fires or overtime. Cost = production + holding + workforce adjustment. Same framework for retail DC staffing seasons and 3PL peak waves.

Problem B — GTA–Great Lakes DC network roles

Logistics · Retail · Distribution Oakville · Mississauga · Buffalo · Detroit

Statement: Should Buffalo act as a cross-dock for US Midwest demand while Mississauga / Oakville serve Ontario, or should Detroit absorb more volume? Constraints: DC capacity, border premium on some lanes, and metro service times.

Approach: Capacity-constrained lowest-cost assignment (see Network Lab below). Design insight: role of each node (fulfillment vs cross-dock) matters as much as raw miles.

Interactive result lives in the Network Optimizer lab — optimal tons by DC, cost-vs-distance lanes, and shipment plan for synthetic NA demand.

3. Supply Chain Dynamics

SCY

How systems behave over time: demand amplification (bullwhip), lead-time and batching effects, information delay, and resilience under disruption. Dynamics explain why a small retail wobble in Toronto can look like a plant crisis in Michigan — and how visibility and shorter feedback loops dampen it.

Skills demonstrated

  • Diagnose bullwhip drivers (batching, forecasting, rationing, lead time)
  • Quantify order amplification across retailer → DC → plant
  • Link information sharing and lead-time reduction to stability

Sample problems

Problem A — Bullwhip on a Canada–US CPG corridor

CPG · Retail · Manufacturing Store → Mississauga DC → Detroit plant

Statement: End-customer demand at GTA stores is relatively smooth. With 2-week ordering cycles, safety buffers, and delayed POS visibility, how much do DC and plant orders amplify over 16 weeks?

Approach: Simulate chained reorder points with forecasting on delayed signals. Compare coefficient of variation (CV) of demand vs orders at each tier. Applies equally to auto parts and grocery.

Problem B — Lead-time shock at the border

Logistics · Cross-border Buffalo corridor

Statement: Mean cross-border transit rises from 1.0 to 2.5 days for two weeks (inspection backlog). What happens to DC inventory and fill rate if policies are unchanged?

Approach: Pipeline inventory ↑ with lead time; without SS uplift, fill rate drops until policy resets. Resilience = detect → buffer or re-route (e.g., more Mississauga domestic fill).

Pipeline stock roughly scales with lead time (+150% in this shock). Holding fill rate requires temporary SS or mode shift — a dynamics problem every NA logistics and retail network faces during corridor incidents.

4. Supply Chain Analytics

SCA

Turning data into decisions: optimization models, single-period (newsvendor) stocking, inventory analytics, and KPI packs (OTIF, cost/ton, turns, capacity). Analytics connects fundamentals and design to measurable outcomes — including dollars on the P&L and cash tied up in working capital.

Skills demonstrated

  • Formulate capacity-constrained allocation / product-mix LPs
  • Apply newsvendor critical-ratio stocking for seasonal / perishable demand
  • Score financial impact of inventory and service decisions (see Cash / WC panel)

Sample problems

Problem A — Capacity-constrained product mix (Mississauga plant)

Manufacturing · Industrial · CPG Two SKUs · shared capacity

Statement: A Mississauga line can make Product H (margin $80/unit) and Product S (margin $200/unit). Weekly capacity ≤ 110 units. Additive A uses 3H+2S ≤ 300; additive B uses 1H+3S ≤ 280. Maximize weekly margin.

Approach: Linear program (original NA instance of a classic product-mix pattern). Solve at binding constraints; report optimal mix and dual pressure on scarce resources.

Problem B — Newsvendor / single-period buy (GTA seasonal SKU)

Retail · CPG · Seasonal Critical ratio · salvage

Statement: A seasonal outdoor / promo SKU for GTA retailers: demand ~ Normal(μ=1,200, σ=280). Cost $22, sell $48, end-of-season salvage $10. How many units to buy before the season?

Approach: Critical ratio CR = Cu / (Cu+Co) where Cu = p−c (underage), Co = c−s (overage). Q* = μ + zCR·σ. Same logic for fashion, fresh CPG, and event-driven manufacturing kits.

Problem C — Network landed-cost minimization

Logistics · Retail · Distribution Multi-DC OR

Statement: Allocate tons from five DCs to ten metros under capacity limits, minimizing freight with border premiums on selected Canada–US lanes.

Approach: Greedy / LP-style lowest-cost assignment with capacity caps — implemented in the Network Lab with live Plotly KPIs.

See Network Optimizer for tons by DC, cost-vs-distance scatter, and top shipment lanes.

5. Supply Chain Technology and Systems

SCT

Digital backbone: ERP, WMS, TMS, visibility towers, and analytics platforms. Technology only creates value when master data, process design, and exception workflows match the physical network — especially across Canada–US handoffs where status latency drives bullwhip and OTIF misses.

Skills demonstrated

  • Map system roles (ERP plan → WMS execute → TMS move → tower monitor)
  • Define OTIF and exception KPIs that ops and IT both own
  • Prioritize integration where information delay is the bottleneck

Sample problems

Problem A — OTIF control tower for Great Lakes lanes

Logistics · Retail · 3PL Visibility · KPI design

Statement: Leadership wants a single tower for Canada–US lanes. Which KPIs, grain, and alert rules catch failure early without flooding planners?

Approach: OTIF by lane + DC, dwell hours at border, forecast bias, and capacity utilization. Alert on OTIF < 92% or border dwell > 8h for 2 consecutive days.

Problem B — WMS ↔ TMS integration priority

Systems · Manufacturing · Distribution Integration ROI

Statement: Oakville WMS and carrier TMS are loosely coupled (nightly files). Cross-border appointment misses cost ~$18k/month in detention + OTIF penalties. Is real-time ASN / appointment sync justified?

Approach: Compare monthly penalty + planner overtime vs integration run-rate. Same business case pattern for retail DC yard management and plant shipping desks.

At $18k/month leakage vs ~$6k/month integration + support, payback is under one quarter if ASN accuracy > 97%. Technology value = closing the information delay that Dynamics module showed amplifies orders.

Cash conversion cycle

CCC = DIO + DSO − DPO. Inventory days, receivables, and payables set how long cash is locked in the chain.

SC → cash levers

Inventory build vs stockout, payment terms with suppliers / customers, and expediting all move CCC and free cash.

P&L bridge

Revenue, COGS, carrying cost, and service / penalty cost show why “lean” is not automatically cheaper.

Decision language

Same story for manufacturing, retail, CPG, and 3PL: trade service risk for cash release (or the reverse).

Working capital — GTA distributor snapshot

Inventory · AR · AP Oakville / Mississauga ops

Statement: Annual COGS $48M, sales $72M. Average inventory $6.4M, AR $7.8M, AP $5.1M. What are DIO, DSO, DPO, and CCC? If we cut DIO by 8 days (better turns) without hurting DSO/DPO, how much cash is released?

Approach: DIO = Inv / (COGS/365); DSO = AR / (Sales/365); DPO = AP / (COGS/365); CCC = DIO+DSO−DPO. Cash release ≈ ΔDIO × (COGS/365).

Scenario — inventory build vs lean (cash vs service)

Cash flow · P&L Interactive

Statement: Compare a buffer plan (higher inventory, lower stockouts) vs a lean plan (lower inventory, more expedites / lost sales). Which improves annual operating contribution after carrying and service costs?

Approach: Waterfall from revenue → COGS → gross margin → carrying − service/penalty → contribution. Toggle scenarios; watch cash tied in inventory and net contribution move together.

How SC decisions move cash

1
Inventory policy — EOQ / SS / newsvendor set DIO; level aggregate plans build stock ahead of peaks (cash out, service up).
2
Payment terms — Longer supplier DPO frees cash; longer customer DSO consumes it. Cross-border NA terms often differ by entity.
3
Expediting & penalties — Rush freight and OTIF penalties are P&L hits that “lean” inventory can trigger when variability is high.
4
Investment lens — Optional NPV framing: inventory reduction projects compete with automation / WMS ROI using the same cash-flow discipline.
